Historical Context

May 14, 1940. Rotterdam's historic center was erased in 90 minutes.

"The Luftwaffe dropped 97 tonnes of incendiary bombs. By nightfall, 260 hectares of medieval city were ash."

The Rotterdam Blitz forced one of the most ambitious urban reconstruction projects in European history. Where narrow canal-side streets once stood, modernist planners drew wide boulevards and vertical towers. The rebuilding wasn't merely restoration, it was reinvention from the ground up.

This accident of history is permanently encoded in the city's elevation data. AHN4 LiDAR captures the height signature of reconstruction: low pre-war structures at the urban periphery, dense mid-century modernist slabs in the former bombed zone, and contemporary high-rises clustered around the Maas waterfront.

The nDSM reveals this stratification with millimetre precision, each building's height above ground is a vote cast in time, recording the era in which Rotterdam decided to build tall rather than horizontal.

Buildings post-1945 dominate Rotterdam's skyline, a direct consequence of Luftwaffe bombing that destroyed the pre-war city centre in a single afternoon.

Data Pipeline

From LiDAR point cloud
to building heights

Six reproducible stages transform raw AHN4 tiles into a GeoJSON with per-building height attributes, ready for 3D web rendering.

01
AHN4 Download Download

PDOK tile service provides 0.5 m resolution rasters covering the entire Netherlands. Rotterdam extent retrieved as GeoTIFF tiles referenced to NAP (Normaal Amsterdams Peil). AHN4 achieves ~10 points/m², roughly 4× the density of AHN3.

# Fetch AHN4 DSM tile via PDOK OGC API curl "https://ns_ahn4.pdok.nl/ogc/tiles/\ ?LAYER=dsm&TILEMATRIXSET=EPSG_28992&\ TILEMATRIX=7&TILEROW=43&TILECOL=51" \ -o ahn4_dsm_rotterdam.tif
02
DSM Processing Raster

Digital Surface Model: the highest elevation return per pixel. Includes buildings, trees, bridges, and any raised infrastructure. Values are metres above NAP. Range in Rotterdam study area: −1.0 to 147.1 m.

import rasterio import numpy as np dsm = rasterio.open("ahn4_dsm_rotterdam.tif") arr_dsm = dsm.read(1) # shape (H, W) nodata = dsm.nodata # typically -9999 arr_dsm[arr_dsm == nodata] = np.nan # Values in metres above NAP datum
03
DTM Processing Raster

Digital Terrain Model: bare-earth ground elevation only. The AHN4 processing pipeline uses ground-classified returns and interpolation to remove buildings and vegetation. Range in Rotterdam: −1.0 to 4.7 m (the city sits largely below sea level).

dtm = rasterio.open("ahn4_dtm_rotterdam.tif") arr_dtm = dtm.read(1) # bare earth surface arr_dtm[arr_dtm == dtm.nodata] = np.nan # Ground elevation: mostly -0.5 to +3 m above NAP # Rotterdam sits in a reclaimed polder
04
nDSM Computation Compute

Normalized DSM = DSM − DTM. Removing the terrain surface eliminates the effect of ground slope, leaving only height above ground. Negative noise (interpolation artefacts) is clipped to zero. This is the key derived layer: nDSM pixel value ≈ building height.

# Subtract terrain from surface model ndsm = arr_dsm - arr_dtm ndsm[ndsm < 0] = 0 # clip negative artefacts ndsm = np.nan_to_num(ndsm, nan=0) # Write result to GeoTIFF preserving CRS with rasterio.open("ndsm.tif", "w", driver="GTiff", crs=dsm.crs, transform=dsm.transform, **profile) as dst: dst.write(ndsm, 1)
05
Zonal Statistics Zonal Stats

BAG (Basisregistratie Adressen en Gebouwen) building footprints used as analysis zones. rasterstats computes mean, max, and std of nDSM pixels falling within each polygon. Mean nDSM is chosen as the height estimate, more robust than max against antenna masts and roof ornaments.

from rasterstats import zonal_stats import geopandas as gpd buildings_gdf = gpd.read_file("bag_buildings.gpkg") stats = zonal_stats( buildings_gdf, "ndsm.tif", stats=["mean", "max", "std"], geojson_out=True ) buildings_gdf["height"] = [ s["mean"] for s in stats ]
06
Web Delivery Export

GeoJSON exported in WGS84 (EPSG:4326) for Mapbox GL JS compatibility. Mapbox fill-extrusion layer renders buildings as 3D geometry directly in the browser, with height-based colour interpolation matching the nDSM colour scale.

# Reproject from RD New to WGS84 for web buildings_wgs84 = buildings_gdf.to_crs("EPSG:4326") buildings_wgs84.to_file( "buildings_heights_wgs84.geojson", driver="GeoJSON" ) # → Mapbox GL JS: fill-extrusion-height: ['get','height']

06
Engineering Decisions

Why these choices?

Each methodological decision involves a trade-off. Here's the reasoning behind the four non-obvious choices in this pipeline.

Decision 01

nDSM over raw DSM

Rotterdam sits in a polder, ground elevation varies from −2 m to +4 m across the study area. Using raw DSM values as building height would conflate terrain slope with building height: a 10 m building on −1 m ground would appear shorter than a 9 m building on +3 m ground. Subtracting the DTM normalises everything to height above local terrain.

→ nDSM = DSM − DTM removes terrain signal
Decision 02

Mean over max zonal stat

Max nDSM within a footprint catches antennas, exhaust stacks, rooftop plant rooms, and LiDAR noise spikes, none of which represent the building's occupied height. Mean nDSM is more representative of the actual floor plate area and produces smoother 3D extrusions. Std deviation is retained as a quality flag: high std suggests a mixed-height footprint or vegetation contamination.

→ mean nDSM ≈ representative occupied height
Decision 03

EPSG:28992 (RD New) for processing

All raster processing runs in RD New, the Dutch national projection. It's metre-based, minimises distortion for the Netherlands, and is the native CRS of AHN4 deliverables. Converting to WGS84 happens only at the final export step, ensuring the zonal statistics run in consistent Cartesian coordinates rather than angular degrees.

→ process in RD New, export in WGS84
Decision 04

AHN4 over AHN3

AHN3 provides ~6, 10 points/m² depending on tile. AHN4 guarantees ≥10 points/m² uniformly across the Netherlands, with improved beam divergence settings that produce sharper building edge delineation. For small footprints (e.g. Amsterdam canal houses) the difference in zonal statistics quality is significant. AHN4 also uses a newer radiometric calibration.

→ AHN4: 10 pts/m², sharper edge delineation
07
Limitations

Honest caveats

Spatial analysis always involves simplifications. These are the known limitations of this methodology.

△
Vegetation confusion Trees within or adjacent to building footprints inflate nDSM values. Tall street trees bordering narrow footprints can cause overestimation by 5, 15 m.
△
Footprint mismatch BAG polygons don't always match the LiDAR acquisition date. Demolished or newly built structures between survey epochs create artefacts.
△
Flat-roof assumption Mean nDSM assumes buildings are roughly flat-roofed. Steeply pitched roofs (common in pre-war Dutch architecture) will be underestimated relative to their ridge height.
△
Scan angle effects LiDAR returns near flight strip edges have higher scan angles, reducing point density and increasing range error. Tall buildings at edge positions may show slight height bias.
△
Sample size This GeoJSON contains ~1,100 buildings from the study area, a subset of Rotterdam's ~30,000 total footprints. Summary statistics will differ from the full-city dataset.
△
Bridge and infrastructure The Erasmus Bridge and elevated motorways are captured by DSM but not filtered from BAG footprints. Some anomalous height values near infrastructure reflect this.
